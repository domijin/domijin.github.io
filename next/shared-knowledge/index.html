WHAT CHANGES THROUGH AN EXCHANGE?

Shared vocabulary is not mutual understanding

1. Common topic

Maya and Leo both recognise Access and Practice. On a map, their contours reach working familiarity on the same spoke, yet Maya means time, quiet space, and a shared phone while Leo means a page that loads. Overlap on a label only marks where a check needs to happen.

2. Understanding a perspective

Can I explain your point in a way you recognise? Here, each person tries to interpret a specific note from the other. Dashed marks show this borrowed context. Their starting maps stay unchanged: a brief exchange does not establish lasting mastery.

3. Shared question & method

Aligning on a question coordinates what to test next without forcing agreement on an answer or merging distinct experience. On the map, the joint frontier extends outward as a dashed question link—not a filled territory of settled knowledge.

4. Examined provisional contribution

A contribution can report an encouraging, mixed, or not-supported outcome. What makes it useful to others is keeping its source, scope, contributors, and remaining unknown intact rather than polishing a claim into certainty.

SOURCES & SCOPE

What informed this example

Three primary works informed the questions highlighted by this demonstration. They motivate why explanation, unshared evidence, and active reconstruction are separated here; they do not validate this interface.

  1. Clark & Brennan (1991), Grounding in CommunicationIn Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition, pp. 127–149. Examines how people coordinate content and process across grounding criteria and media. Shared words do not establish mutual understanding; a button here cannot establish another real person's understanding.
  2. Stasser & Titus (1985), Pooling of Unshared Information in Group Decision MakingJournal of Personality and Social Psychology 48(6), 1467–1478. In their caucus simulation, groups often failed to combine unshared corrective facts and discussion favoured shared facts and existing preferences. Making distinctive evidence inspectable does not by itself resolve sampling bias or make every disagreement useful.
  3. Chi & Wylie (2014), The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active Learning OutcomesEducational Psychologist 49(4), 219–243 (DOI). Differentiates modes of cognitive engagement and reviews learning evidence with explicit caveats. Invites active reconstruction rather than passive reveal—while noting that answering a single question here demonstrates neither durable learning nor mastery.

The maps are a schematic view of a circle of competence: six topics, with familiarity assigned for this example. Distance and area are not measured scores. The interaction uses fictional material and prepares a note for this visit only; it does not profile people, assess mastery or publish their contributions.
