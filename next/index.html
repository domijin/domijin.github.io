A Sci-Fi Mind · An Engineer’s Hands

From a Million Orbiting Stars to the Human Sitting Next to You

How computational astrophysics, 50-millisecond decision systems, and Palo Alto field research shaped one question: what helps people think clearly, keep their independence, and build together?

Ten years ago at the University of Texas at Arlington, I spent my days watching a million virtual stars tug on one another inside supercomputer simulations, waiting billions of years of model time for two black holes to spiral together. Since then, my work has moved closer and closer to everyday human life—from pricing and recommendation engines at Uber, Airbnb, and SmartNews, to sitting in passenger seats with older drivers in Palo Alto testing self-driving cars.

Across every change of scale, the habit that works best has stayed the same: sketch a clear picture of how the system works, test it in the real world, and listen honestly when human experience pushes back.

Everything is here in full: 28 essays, field notes and specifications under four threads. The figures are interactive, so you can check the mechanism yourself. Where a piece first ran elsewhere, the original is credited at the end.

“我们都在穷尽自己仅有的一点天赋，来表达内心深处的感受”

We spend our whole lives exhausting our modest gifts to express what lies deep in the soul—and, as my family motto reminds me, “但行好事，莫问前程” (do good work wholeheartedly, without fixating on the reward ahead).

01 · Cosmic Scale · 2013 – 2018

What a Million Stars Taught Me About Patient Systems

Simulating 662,772 globular clusters and 17.88 million binary black holes at UT Arlington—and learning why slow baseline interactions always shape rare breakthroughs.

Look at a globular cluster through a backyard telescope and it appears frozen—a quiet snow globe of a million stars packed into a sphere a few parsecs wide. Speed up the clock to an 800-million-year relaxation time (τ_rh), however, and that snow globe turns into a restless gravitational engine. Heavy black holes lose kinetic energy to lighter stars, sink to the core, and lock into tight binary dances.

In my doctoral work at UT Arlington, I ran and synthesized 3,240 MOCCA cluster simulations across 8,946 galaxies within 30 Mpc. That cosmic census revealed 17.88 million inspiralling binary black holes, a local merger rate of η ≃ 587 Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹, and 17 LISA “standard sirens” pinpointed tightly enough (ΔV ≤ 1 Mpc³) to measure the expansion of the universe independently of starlight.

02 · System Scale · 2018 – 2026

Making Sense in 50 Milliseconds—From Red Lights to Ad Auctions

Waiting at an empty morning intersection, pricing rides and rooms at Uber and Airbnb, and decoupling fast reflex serving (<50 ms) from slow, thoughtful coordination.

Sitting at a red light at an empty intersection one morning, I found myself doing the math: why do cities still run intersections on blind, fixed timers when approaching cars already carry GPS sensors? Even if only a fraction of vehicles share telemetry, pairing that sparse data with physical conservation laws (like Stephen Wolfram’s symbolic-neural hybrids) lets a system infer queue lengths and clear traffic dynamically.

I ran into the exact same structural puzzle working on marketplace pricing and advertising systems at Uber, Airbnb, and SmartNews. When you have less than 50 milliseconds to serve a decision, you cannot run deep reasoning on the hot path—and if you only optimize for immediate clicks, you burn out user trust. The solution is a two-speed architecture: a lightweight, safety-bounded reflex lane (<50 ms p99) steered by an asynchronous 8-agent reflection loop that recalibrates real user demand and creative fatigue every few minutes.

03 · Human Scale · 2024 – 2026

Designing AI That Keeps Us in the Driver’s Seat

Riding in self-driving Teslas with Palo Alto seniors, reclaiming play as lifelong curiosity, and building local tools that sharpen human judgment instead of replacing it.

In late 2024 I stepped outside pure software loops to start Kin Domi in Palo Alto—spending afternoons in living rooms, community centers, and passenger seats with older adults. Watching seniors use Tesla’s Full Self-Driving (FSD) showed me something no benchmark captures: older drivers have the most to gain from autonomous mobility, yet cockpit alerts are built for thirty-year-old engineers. Sudden takeover chimes and tiny icons create panic at the exact moment calm reassurance is needed.

When I wrote Reclaiming Play as a Life Skill, several older readers gently told me they disliked the word “play” because it sounded childish. That conversation reshaped how I build: what people want at every age is dignified self-reliance, low-stakes curiosity, and a partner that respects their judgment—whether in Open Learning Lab, baby-AI, or iMessage AI Router.

04 · Civic Scale · 2018 – Present

Shared Values (GCD), Complementary Skills (LCM), and Rhythm

Why engineering cultures soar or stall, how eight years of color-coded calendars keep life whole, and how shared ethics let diverse minds build a larger future.

Why do some teams compound curiosity into generational breakthroughs while others with ten times the capital stall? Whether comparing Boeing and SpaceX, studying eVTOL airworthiness across the US and China, or reflecting on our own responsibilities as builders, the bottleneck is rarely raw IQ. It is whether incentives reward first-principles honesty over short-term optics.

In my Substack essays and the civic notebook LibertyEarth, I use a simple mathematical picture for healthy collaboration: treat shared ethical values as the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) that keeps baseline trust high, and treat complementary human + AI skills as the Least Common Multiple (LCM) that expands what we can build together—anchored day to day by my 8th-iteration color-coded weekly rhythm.
