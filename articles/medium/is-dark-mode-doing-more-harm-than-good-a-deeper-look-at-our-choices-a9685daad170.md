# Is Dark Mode Doing More Harm Than Good? A Deeper Look at Our Choices

*Medium · Product & Ergonomics · November 2024 — By Domi Jin*

I recently saw a post from a friend saying that dark mode was causing significant eye strain. He had to lean in closer to see clearly, which eventually led to neck issues. This got me thinking about the growing debate: is dark mode, originally designed to reduce blue light exposure and improve our digital habits, actually harmful to our eyes? After a quick search, I found mixed opinions (e.g., [ophthalmology24](https://www.ophthalmology24.com/using-dark-mode)). I also explored studies on PubMed using keywords like Dark Mode, Eye Strain, and Visual Fatigue, and realized that **there isn’t much robust, statistically significant research** to fully understand its health impacts, especially when it comes to guiding our work-style decisions.

### Why Was Dark Mode Introduced?

Dark mode was introduced to reduce blue light exposure, which is believed to interfere with sleep patterns. While it may help in reducing blue light, it has also introduced unintended consequences, such as increased visual fatigue for some users. This led me to wonder — have we really thought deeply enough about the features we adopt so quickly?

### Evaluating Dark Mode: Original Pros vs. Hidden Cons

Below is a comparison highlighting the original anticipated benefits of dark mode versus the hidden drawbacks discovered later:

| Dimension | Original Anticipated Benefits (Pros) | Hidden Ergonomic Drawbacks (Cons) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Visual Ergonomics & Astigmatism** | Reduced blue-light emission at night; lower perceived screen glare in dark rooms. | Pupil dilation in bright/daylight settings triggers spherical aberration and halation (“halo” blur), especially for users with astigmatism. |
| **Data Density & Visual Hierarchy** | Sleek, high-contrast aesthetic for media playback, code syntax highlighting, and sparse dashboards. | Reduced contrast sensitivity for dense black-on-white enterprise tables, long-form text, and multi-layered elevation shadows. |
| **Context Switching & Posture** | Comfortable for isolated nighttime browsing and OLED battery conservation. | Pupillary adaptation fatigue when switching between dark apps and bright documents/spreadsheets; users lean forward to resolve blurred edges, causing neck strain. |
| **Ideal Use Cases** | Low-light environments, short UI interactions, evening media consumption, and terminal/IDE workflows in dim rooms. | Poor default for well-lit daytime offices, extended analytical reading, and mixed-window enterprise workflows. |

**Note:** The original pros show that dark mode is particularly beneficial in low-light environments. However, many hidden cons were later discovered, especially impacting readability and comfort in well-lit conditions.

### The Need for a Structured Decision Workflow

- Were the pros and cons of dark mode already known during the research and feature experimentation phase? Did we fully understand the implications before launching this feature?
- Was there any design consideration to guide users during feature onboarding, helping them make appropriate decisions? Could we have made this process more automatic — switching modes based on the light environment — and educating users on why the switch happens?
- Below is a reference decision framework that could help users determine when to adopt dark mode where it is beneficial:

#### Decision Workflow for Users

**Step 1: Assess Environment**

- **Low-Light Settings**: Favor dark mode to reduce glare and enhance comfort.
- **Bright or Well-Lit Settings**: Favor light mode for improved readability.

**Step 2: Evaluate Usage Needs**

- **Reading Text**: Prefer light mode for better contrast and focus.
- **Short Interactions or UI Navigation**: Dark mode may be more visually appealing.

**Step 3: Health and Vision Considerations**

- **Sleep Concerns**: Use dark mode at night to minimize blue light exposure.
- **Eye Strain Sensitivity**: Choose light mode for better visual clarity during extended screen use.

### The Pros and Cons of Agile Decision Making

Agile decision making has become a dominant approach in many industries, enabling teams to build, test, and iterate quickly. However, this approach comes with both advantages and drawbacks.

#### Pros of Agile in Decision Making

- **Flexibility and Adaptability**: Agile allows for fast iteration based on feedback, which helps teams adjust to changing requirements and user needs.
- **User-Centric Development**: The emphasis on testing and iterating puts users at the center of the development process, allowing for immediate feedback incorporation.
- **Reduced Risk of Big Failures**: By breaking down development into smaller parts and iterating, the risk associated with a large-scale product launch is reduced.

#### Cons of Agile in Decision Making

- **Short-Term Focus**: Agile tends to prioritize short-term gains and immediate iterations, sometimes at the expense of a long-term vision.
- **Potential Lack of Research**: Quick iterations may lead to insufficient in-depth research and understanding before implementing features, leading to hidden issues surfacing later.
- **Pressure to Deliver Quickly**: The emphasis on speed can lead to rushed decisions and shortcuts, which may impact overall quality.

### Decision Matrix: Perspectives on Agile Decision Making

Below is a decision matrix reflecting the perspectives of different teams and user personas on why they might be incentivized to build, test, and iterate quickly rather than follow a more traditional research, development, and release model:

| Stakeholder / User Persona | Primary Environment & Session Duration | Incentive to Ship Fast (Short-Term Gain) | Long-Term Hidden Cost & Recommended Default |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Product & Engineering Teams** | Dimly lit dev setups; 6–10 hr coding/UI sessions | Ship visible roadmap features rapidly; match industry trendiness and developer preference. | Skipped longitudinal ergonomic testing; maintaining dual-theme token parity. *Recommended: Auto-ambient schedule.* |
| **Marketing & Growth Teams** | Cross-channel demos; brief high-impact previews | High visual novelty in launch screenshots; immediate social buzz and feature-adoption spikes. | User churn or silent fatigue when aesthetic novelty wears off during daily work. *Recommended: Context-aware onboarding.* |
| **Leadership & Investors** | Executive dashboards; 30–60 min review blocks | Demonstrate execution velocity and competitive parity at minimal upfront R&D cost. | Accumulated UX debt and lower enterprise productivity across thousands of seat hours. *Recommended: Research-backed defaults.* |
| **Enterprise Knowledge Workers** | Bright office (300–500 lux); 4–8 hr dense reading & docs | Instant visual relief from harsh uncalibrated monitor brightness; perceived modern UI. | Halation blur, pupil constriction fatigue when switching windows, and forward-lean neck strain. *Recommended: Light Mode default in daylight.* |

This decision matrix highlights that while building, testing, and iterating offer flexibility and user-driven changes, they also introduce risks associated with insufficient research and rushed implementation.

### A Universal Problem: Short-Term vs. Long-Term Thinking

This issue isn’t limited to people in certain roles or to the tech industry — it’s a universal one. Many of us, whether builders, suppliers, marketers, or consumers, contribute to a fast-paced, instant gratification-driven market. We prioritize short-term comfort over long-term value, reinforcing a cycle that pushes speed as the ultimate goal. Products, companies, investors, and consumers all fall into this pattern, driving a market that is moving at the speed of breaking.

But I believe the game has changed. The real value lies in understanding our collective contributions to this fast-paced market. It’s not just builders, suppliers, and marketers, but also us as consumers. We collectively prioritize speed and short-term gains, which pushes us to the brink of breaking. The true challenge is to know what’s fundamentally good, hold firm to those core principles, and make decisions with a long-term perspective, even in a dynamic and complex world. Whether we’re talking about a product, a company, or our personal lives, this should be our guiding value.
