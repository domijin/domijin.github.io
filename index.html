A Sci-Fi Mind · An Engineer’s Hands

From a Million Orbiting Stars to the Human Sitting Next to You

How computational astrophysics, 50-millisecond decision systems, and Palo Alto field research shaped one question: what helps people think clearly, keep their independence, and build together?

Ten years ago at the University of Texas at Arlington, I spent my days watching a million virtual stars tug on one another inside supercomputer simulations, waiting billions of years of model time for two black holes to spiral together. Since then, my work has moved closer and closer to everyday human life—from recommendation engines that read mobility data, and marketplace pricing that had to survive contact with real demand, to sitting in passenger seats with older drivers in Palo Alto testing self-driving cars.

Across every change of scale, the habit that works best has stayed the same: sketch a clear picture of how the system works, test it in the real world, and listen honestly when human experience pushes back.

“我们都在穷尽自己仅有的一点天赋，来表达内心深处的感受”

We spend our whole lives exhausting our modest gifts to express what lies deep in the soul—and, as my family motto reminds me, “但行好事，莫问前程” (do good work wholeheartedly, without fixating on the reward ahead).

01 · Cosmic Scale · 2013 – 2018

The Story of a Million Stars: a Complex Adaptive System

Simulating 662,772 globular clusters and 17.88 million binary black holes at UT Arlington, watching a million stars rearrange themselves, and finding that the same feedback rules traffic, auctions and teams.

Look at a globular cluster through a backyard telescope and it appears frozen—a quiet snow globe of a million stars packed into a sphere a few parsecs wide. Speed up the clock to an 800-million-year relaxation time (τ_rh), however, and that snow globe turns into a restless gravitational engine. Heavy black holes lose kinetic energy to lighter stars, sink to the core, and lock into tight binary dances.

In my doctoral work at UT Arlington, I ran and synthesized 3,240 MOCCA cluster simulations across 8,946 galaxies within 30 Mpc. That cosmic census revealed 17.88 million inspiralling binary black holes, a local merger rate of η ≃ 587 Gpc⁻³ yr⁻¹, and 17 LISA “standard sirens” pinpointed tightly enough (ΔV ≤ 1 Mpc³) to measure the expansion of the universe independently of starlight.

02 · System Scale · 2018 – 2026

Making Sense in 50 Milliseconds—From Red Lights to Ad Auctions

Reverse-engineering a traffic-light countdown, writing down an ad-serving path that keeps its tail latency down without shrinking the model, and asking what the Growth Trap does to the people on the other side of the screen.

Sitting at a red light at an empty intersection one morning, I found myself doing the math: why do cities still run intersections on blind, fixed timers when approaching cars already carry GPS sensors? Even if only a fraction of vehicles share telemetry, pairing that sparse data with physical conservation laws (like Stephen Wolfram’s symbolic-neural hybrids) lets a system infer queue lengths and clear traffic dynamically.

The same structural puzzle came back in advertising. A serving path has to answer inside the latency budget the platform allows, and the trade-off is stated bluntly in Rethinking Digital Advertising: real-time ad platforms cannot tolerate the latency that AppLovin’s models can, and that headroom is exactly what buys larger models and better personalisation. So I wrote the system down instead of the slogan — a real-time ingestion pipeline and feature store feeding a labelling and profile service, which feeds a dynamic ad-serving path with in-session creative switching, backed by distilled user profiles so candidate generation stays cheap.

The rest of the argument matters more than the plumbing. If you optimise only for immediate clicks, you exhaust the audience: that is the Growth Trap, where consumer fatigue, regulatory risk and competitive erosion meet. A system that serves a decision in milliseconds still has to answer the slower question underneath it — whether the decision is worth making at all.

03 · Human Scale · 2024 – 2026

Designing AI That Keeps Us in the Driver’s Seat

Riding in self-driving Teslas with Palo Alto seniors, reclaiming play as lifelong curiosity, and building local tools that sharpen human judgment instead of replacing it.

In late 2024 I stepped outside pure software loops to start Kin Domi in Palo Alto—spending afternoons in living rooms, community centers, and passenger seats with older adults. Watching seniors use Tesla’s Full Self-Driving (FSD) showed me something no benchmark captures: older drivers have the most to gain from autonomous mobility, yet cockpit alerts are built for thirty-year-old engineers. Sudden takeover chimes and tiny icons create panic at the exact moment calm reassurance is needed.

When I wrote Reclaiming Play as a Life Skill, several older readers gently told me they disliked the word “play” because it sounded childish. That conversation reshaped how I build: what people want at every age is dignified self-reliance, low-stakes curiosity, and a partner that respects their judgment—whether in Open Learning Lab, baby-AI, or iMessage AI Router.

04 · Civic Scale · 2018 – Present

Collective Competence: One Mission, Every Person Whole

From human capital in an ‘organized farm’ to organizing intelligence with empowered individuals — and a proposed measure of a resilient, capable team: collective competence with mission alignment, holding diversity and individuality at once.

Why do some teams compound curiosity into generational breakthroughs while others with ten times the capital stall? Whether comparing Boeing and SpaceX, studying eVTOL airworthiness across the US and China, or reflecting on our own responsibilities as builders, the bottleneck is rarely raw IQ. It is whether incentives reward first-principles honesty over short-term optics.

In my Substack essays and the civic notebook LibertyEarth, I use a simple mathematical picture for healthy collaboration: treat shared ethical values as the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) that keeps baseline trust high, and treat complementary human + AI skills as the Least Common Multiple (LCM) that expands what we can build together—anchored day to day by the eighth revision of the work model I keep in my founder's log.

Shared values and complementary skills are the mechanism; what they are for is collective competence. The institutions I have worked in still organize people the way an organized farm organizes capital: interchangeable units, measured by output, replaceable when the season turns. That shape does not survive the move to organizing intelligence with empowered individuals. So I am trying to measure a resilient, capable team instead: collective competence — what the group can do that none of its members could do alone, held together by one mission, and sustained by leaving every person's difference intact. All three have to hold at once. Take the mission away and the team is a queue; take the individuality away and it is a crowd. The Avengers are the reference case — nobody interchangeable, everybody committed to the same thing.
